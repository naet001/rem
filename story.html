A Short Story

The Lighthouse at the Edge of the World

by an anonymous keeper of small flames

The lighthouse had stood on the cliff for three hundred years, and for three hundred years no one had asked why the light was lit. The village below — a scatter of slate roofs and salt-scoured windows — had long ago stopped wondering. The light was simply there, the way the tide was there and the gulls were there, turning in the grey air above the harbour like a thought that could not quite be finished.

The keeper, an old woman named Mara, had climbed the spiral stairs every evening since she was nineteen. She did not trim the wick with oil and brass like the keepers of old; the lamp ran on electricity now, humming in its glass chamber like a trapped sun. But Mara still climbed. The climb was a prayer, and prayers, she believed, ought to cost something.

One November night, a ship appeared that had no business being there. The shipping lanes ran forty miles south, past the cold teeth of the shallows, and no captain in his right mind would bring a vessel this close to the cliffs in a fog like wet wool. Yet there it was — a dark hull, and a single light at its mast, burning oddly green.

Mara watched it from the gallery. The ship did not move toward the harbour, nor away. It simply sat, riding the swell, as if waiting for something it could not name.

She was not afraid. Keeper lore was full of such ships — the story said they came for those who had lit the lamp faithfully and would soon leave it behind. But Mara had heard that story since she was a girl who could barely reach the first stair, and she had never believed it. Belief, she thought, was for people who had not spent forty years alone with a machine and a sea.

The next evening the ship was closer, riding just beyond the foaming rim of the rocks, and a second light burned green at its bow. The evening after that, it was close enough that Mara could see the shape of a figure standing at the rail — small, still, watching the lamp as the lamp watched it back.

On the fourth night she left the gallery early, and climbed instead down the seaward stair that no one used anymore, to the ledge where the spray came up like rain in the wind. The ship had come to rest against the rock, gentle as a hand closing around a coin. The figure at the rail was a girl. She wore no coat, no hat, nothing against the cold.

"You've been waiting," the girl said. Her voice carried strangely over the water, as if it came from much closer than she stood. "Three nights now. Most people don't wait."

"I wasn't sure you were real," Mara said. "And I wasn't sure I wanted to find out."

The girl smiled, and for a moment the green light softened, warming to the colour of a kerosene flame. "I am the last of the old ships," she said. "I have been looking for the lamp that was lit by hand." She looked up at the tower, at the steady white beam sweeping the fog. "This one hums. It has forgotten the old language."

Mara stood on the ledge a long time after the ship glided away into the dark. In her pocket, the brass matchbox her grandfather had given her on the day she took the post. Forty years, and she had never opened it. The lamp had never needed it.

That night, for the first time, she climbed the spiral stairs, opened the glass chamber, and struck a match. The little flame burned yellow and honest, and the electric lamp, startled, went on humming as if the world had not changed. But Mara knew better.

Somewhere out on the water, a dark ship with a green light turned — slowly, surely, like a compass needle finding the truth — and came home.

In the morning the village found the lamp burning brighter than it had in a century, and no one could say why, and no one dared to change it.

The End
